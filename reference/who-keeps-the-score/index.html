The tissue keeps the score.

A crushed wrist still hurts seven months later. Every explanation you write for that pain is a claim about where the score is being kept, and every herb, dose and referral you choose afterwards has to answer to that claim.

Start with the introduction, or read the case for the tissue.

R., seven months after a crushed wrist

Who
41, right-handed, self-employed carpenter.
Injury
Right wrist caught in a hydraulic press: distal radius fracture with soft-tissue crush. Plate fixation on day 6.
Course
Week-one pain scores of 8 to 9 out of 10. Out of the cast at 6 weeks; fracture united on X-ray at 10 weeks.
Now
Burning, aching pain over the back of the hand and wrist, worse at night and in the cold. Grip is weak and R. avoids using the hand. Sleep is broken. Not working.

Where the title comes from

The title borrows from Bessel van der Kolk's The Body Keeps the Score, a seminal work on trauma and its healing. The book's title is a claim about location: trauma is held in the body as well as the mind, and healing has to engage both.

Chronic pain poses the same question with more candidates. When a crushed wrist still hurts months after the bone has healed, the score could be kept in the injured tissue, the nerve endings, the spinal cord, the brain, the person's expectations, the immune system, the history of the injury, or the world the person lives in. Most real cases involve several. A useful formulation says which ones, how they connect, and what that means for the remedy, the dose and the referral.

This page supports that work. It is written for herbal practitioners and students who reach for a biopsychosocial explanation of chronic pain and then have to turn it into a plan a colleague, a co-managing physician or the patient could check.

Who keeps the score?

Herbal pain formulations often reach for "biopsychosocial" and then write three separate paragraphs: one on tissue, one on stress, one on work, each with its own herb. That arrangement never answers the first question a formulation has to answer: where is this pain being generated and kept going? The 2020 IASP definition is the place to start. It separates pain, a personal experience, from nociception, the neural encoding of noxious events, and it says either can occur without the other (Raja et al., 2020).

Each keeper below is a live research program. Choose one and read what it claims, what it predicts for R., and what it cannot explain. The lines in the ring are documented mechanisms that connect two levels. A biopsychosocial account worth the name is built out of those lines (Cormack et al., 2023).

Bridges from here

    Commit test

    Pick an intervention that often turns up in herbal pain plans. The verdict depends on the keeper chosen above. An intervention is licensed when it acts where your stance says the score is kept. It needs a stated bridge when it acts one documented link away. It is incoherent as written when nothing connects the two directly.

    Choose an intervention to see whether your stance supports it.

    The receptor bench

    A mechanistic claim names a molecule, a place and an exposure. The bench lays out the main molecules on the path from R.'s injured hand to the spinal dorsal horn and up into the brain. Select one to see what it detects, how it signals, what sensitizes it, and which plant compounds have measured activity there. The evidence tag says what kind of evidence it is: cells, animals or people.

    Test the hand

    Switch states on and off, then apply a stimulus to the back of R.'s hand. Around an injury, heat and pinprick both hurt more where the damage is (primary hyperalgesia). In the wider zone around it only mechanical stimuli are amplified (secondary hyperalgesia), because that amplification happens in the spinal cord (Woolf, 2011). That difference is a quick bedside clue to where the score is being kept.

    Dose makes the claim

    Posology is the whole prescription: dose, frequency, duration, preparation and route. A dose is therapeutic for a mechanism when it plausibly puts enough active compound at the target for long enough. Subtherapeutic means short of that, for that mechanism. The same ten drops can be therapeutic for a bitter reflex on the tongue and subtherapeutic for prostaglandin synthesis in a wrist.

    Strength is half of every dose. "2 mL of willow bark" means nothing until the ratio is written down: 2 mL of a 1:5 tincture carries the extract of 0.4 g of bark, and 2 mL of a 1:2 liquid extract carries 1 g.

      Drop arithmetic

      A drop is a volume that depends on the dropper and the liquid. A pharmacopoeial dropper is calibrated so that 20 drops of water make about 1 mL. Ethanolic tinctures have lower surface tension and form smaller drops, so the same count delivers less. Prescribe in mL, or measure your dropper.

      Why drop doses get prescribed

      Small doses have several rationales. Some carry a mechanism and some do not. The tag says which.

        If you use energetics, keep the mechanism.

        Energetic language is a compressed clinical description. Heat, cold, damp, dry, tension and laxity named observable tissue states long before receptors had names, and the physiomedicalists and eclectics who handed the vocabulary down argued from the physiology of their day (Wood, 2004). Ellingwood's 1919 entry on lobelia sets out "Physiological Action" and "Specific Symptomatology" on the same page and gives toxic signs alongside the dose (Ellingwood, 1919).

        Drop the mechanism and the energetic claim stops being checkable. A colleague, a physician co-managing R., or a reviewer cannot tell a careful observation from a guess, and the authority you reached for goes with it. Keep both: state the tissue state, the physiological bridge you think it names, the prediction that would prove you wrong, and a dose that can reach the target.

        Phenotype the wrist

        Before choosing an herb, name what kind of pain R. has. Three mechanistic descriptors are in use. Nociceptive pain arises from actual or threatened damage to non-neural tissue. Neuropathic pain comes from a lesion or disease of the somatosensory nervous system. Nociplastic pain arises from altered nociception without clear evidence of either (Kosek et al., 2016). They can coexist in one person.

        A crushed wrist raises a question herbal pain formulations rarely ask: is this complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS)? In 1,549 people treated without surgery for a wrist fracture, 3.8% developed CRPS, and pain above 5 out of 10 in the first week was the warning sign (Moseley et al., 2014). R. had surgery and reported 8 to 9. Tick the findings you would elicit, or load an example presentation, and watch the profile and the licensed interventions change.

        The criteria follow the Budapest clinical criteria (Harden et al., 2010) with the Valencia update (Goebel et al., 2021), the neuropathic grading system (Finnerup et al., 2016), and the nociplastic criteria (Kosek et al., 2021). This is a teaching tool. It does not diagnose anyone.
