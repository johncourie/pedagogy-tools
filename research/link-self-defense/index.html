A field guide to links, citations and privacy

Link Self‑Defense

Links often carry more than an address. They can record where you found a page, who shared it with you, and sometimes who you are. This guide shows you how to read a link, how to cite one correctly in APA Style, and how to share one without sending that extra information along.

Paste any link or DOI. The reader takes it apart, explains each piece, and gives you two results: a clean link you can share, and the form APA 7 asks for in a reference list. If the terms are new to you, start with How links work.

    How links work

    A web address, or URL, has up to five parts. Most pages can be reached with only the first three.

    Scheme
    How your browser connects to the site. Use https whenever it works.
    Host
    The site itself. Check it: a familiar name followed by an unfamiliar domain usually means the link has been routed through something else.
    Path
    The page on that site.
    Query
    Extra information after the ?, written as name=value pairs joined by &.
    Fragment
    A spot within the page, after the #. Your browser uses it to jump to that spot, and the site never receives it.

    The query string

    Some parameters in the query tell the site what to show. Remove a YouTube link’s v= and the site no longer knows which video you meant. Many others exist only to record how you arrived. The reader sorts parameters into five kinds.

    Labelutm_source, utm_medium, ref, via, feature

    Says where you came from: a newsletter, a social site, a search, an AI tool. The destination’s analytics count it, and anyone who reads the link can see it.

    Strip
    Click IDfbclid, gclid, msclkid, _gl

    A unique tag created for one click. Ad networks and the destination site can match it to an account and follow it from site to site.

    Strip
    Share IDsi, is, igsh, t on X

    Ties the link to the account that shared it. When your reader opens it, the platform can connect the two of you.

    Strip
    Tokens and personal infocasa_token, sessionid, token, mc_eid, an email address

    Can identify you or let someone into something meant for you. Some break the link for everyone else. Others quietly keep working, which is worse.

    Strip, always
    Contentv, t on YouTube, id, page, q

    Tells the site what to show. Remove it and the link breaks or lands somewhere else.

    Keep

    Read the shape, not the list

    In early 2026 YouTube renamed the share ID on its links from si= to is=. Link cleaners that work from a list of known names let the new one through until their lists caught up. Anyone reading the link would still have recognized it.

    The shape gives these parameters away. A value that is a run of random letters and digits, like is=Xa9k2Lm0Pq7Rt3Zw, is an ID. Treat every ID as tracking until the page proves it needs it: remove it, then test the link. The reader marks unfamiliar IDs as Unknown ID and leaves the decision to you.

    Wrappers and redirects

    Some links are not the destination at all. Many school and workplace email systems rewrite every link so it passes through a scanning service first, and search engines and social sites do something similar. The wrapper hides the real address inside its own, and it can carry details about you, such as the email address the message was sent to. Shortened links such as bit.ly and t.co hide the destination entirely.

    The reader unwraps the common wrappers. For a shortened link, open it and copy the address you land on.

    Next: Citing in APA 7

    Citing links in APA 7

    The seventh edition of APA Style decides which address belongs at the end of a reference. Work down this list and stop at the first case that fits.

    1. The work has a DOI. End the reference with the DOI written as a link, https://doi.org/10.xxxx/xxxxx, and leave the web address out. Include the DOI whether you read the work online or in print.
    2. It is a journal article from a library database (EBSCO, ProQuest, CINAHL and similar) with no DOI. Leave out the URL and the database name, and cite it as you would a print article. These works are widely available, so readers do not need your route to them.
    3. It comes from a database that publishes its own content, such as UpToDate or Natural Medicines. Give the database name and the address of the work. If that address only opens after signing in, or is tied to your session, give the database’s home or sign-in page instead.
    4. It is an ordinary web page. Use the clean address. If the page is designed to change over time and is not archived, add the date you retrieved it.

    Whatever you end up with, open it in a private window before you submit. APA also accepts shortened DOIs and shortened URLs, provided you check that they lead to the right place.

    Formatting DOIs

    Write every DOI in the current form, https://doi.org/ followed by the number, even when the article prints an older form such as doi:10.1037/a0040251 or http://dx.doi.org/10.1037/a0040251. Paste either older form into the reader and it will rewrite it for you.

    Sources found with AI tools

    AI search tools often tag the links they hand you, for example utm_source=chatgpt.com. The tag tells the site, and everyone who reads your reference, where you found the source. Remove it like any other label.

    Then check the source itself. AI tools can produce a citation that looks right and points to a different paper, or to nothing at all. Open the DOI and confirm that the title, authors and year match what you are citing.

    Google Scholar links

    Scholar sometimes adds casa_token to links that open through a campus subscription. It is an access token. Remove it, and use the article’s DOI in your reference.

    Next: Library links

    Library links

    When you read an article through your school’s library, the library is paying the publisher for your access. To confirm that you belong, many libraries route your connection through a proxy server, which signs you in and then fetches the article on your behalf. You can see this in the address bar: the publisher’s address changes to include your library’s.

    Recognizing a proxied link

    • The publisher’s name is written with hyphens and followed by another domain, as in www-sciencedirect-com.ezproxy.library.example.edu.
    • The address contains login?url= or qurl= followed by the real address.
    • The address ends in .idm.oclc.org, or passes through openathens.net.
    • Your browser showed your library’s sign-in page before the article appeared.

    Which link to use where

    In a reference list

    Never the proxied link. It works only for people signed in at your library, and it names your institution. Use the DOI, or, for a database article with no DOI, no URL at all. The reader removes common proxies for you.

    With classmates at your school

    Here the proxied link is often the right one, because it sends classmates through the same sign-in. Better still, use the database’s own Permalink or Share button, which your library may have set up to include the proxy so the link keeps working.

    Finding your library’s proxy

    You rarely need the proxy address itself, but knowing it helps when a link fails. Look on your library’s website for pages titled off-campus access, connecting from home, or proxy bookmarklet. Reference managers such as Zotero can also recognize a library proxy after you sign in through it, and will then route links through it for you.

    Asking a librarian

    Librarians answer questions like this every day, and a specific question gets a quick, specific answer. Check your library’s research guides or FAQ first. If the answer is not there, this message gives a librarian everything needed to help. Fill in the brackets before you send it.

    Message to your library

    Hello, I am a student in [program or course]. I want to share links to articles from [database name] in two ways: with classmates at our school, and in an APA 7 reference list. What is the best way to get a persistent link from [database name]? And what is our library’s proxy address, so I can tell when a link will only work for people signed in through our library? Thank you.

    Next: Self-defense

    Self-defense

    APA Style asks one question about a link: will it get your reader to the source? It does not ask what the link reveals about you, or what it does to your reader when they click. This section is about that second question, and it reflects my position as a member of the Electronic Frontier Foundation.

    What a link can reveal

    • Where you found something. Labels such as utm_source name the newsletter, search engine or AI tool that sent you.
    • Who shared it. Share IDs from YouTube, Instagram, TikTok and X can connect whoever opens the link to the account that shared it.
    • Who you are. Email wrappers and newsletter links can carry your email address or a subscriber ID.
    • What you were reading. Text fragments (#:~:text=) record the exact passage you highlighted.
    • Where you study. Proxied links name your institution.

    Your readers are your allies

    EFF’s Surveillance Self-Defense guide asks you to plan around what you need to protect, from whom, and who your allies are, since your privacy overlaps with theirs. When you cite or share a link, your readers become part of that plan. A share ID in a citation ties every reader’s click back to you, and copied references carry it into other people’s work. Cleaning a link protects you and everyone who follows it. Most professions that handle other people’s information already practice this kind of care. Links deserve the same reflex.

    Habits that last

    • Copy links from the address bar. Share buttons are where share IDs get added.
    • Read before you paste. Everything after the ? is something to decide about.
    • Open links from email before you copy them, and take the address you land on.
    • Run the private-window test on every link you cite.
    • Make your own plan with EFF’s guide, starting with Your Security Plan.
