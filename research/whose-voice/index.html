Whose Voice Is It?

Sorting Traditional Ecological Knowledge, ethnobotany, and the peer-reviewed sources that only look like them.

For a monograph or piece of research you are working on that has an ethnobotanical or TEK section

Who is speaking, and from where?

Every source in this section is sorted by voice, not by richness. A beautiful, detailed, well-cited source can still be the wrong category. A section earns its ethnobotanical and TEK sources by knowing which is which, and it earns them honestly by saying so when one category cannot be found.

TEKKnowledge from an actual population, in its own voice. Myths, oral traditions, community protocols, academic TEK papers written with or by the community.

"We use it this way."

EthnobotanyKnowledge of use related by a middleman (missionary, scientist, colonist) or produced inside the formal discipline with its rules and consensus.

"They use it this way."

Historical medical herbalismKnowledge inside a formal medical tradition: Eclectics, Thomsonians, Physiomedicalists, 19th-century pharmacy.

A different part of the story. It is neither ethnobotany nor TEK.

The four bins you will sort into

Two of them give you a source the section needs. Two of them give you nothing on their own, and the difference between those two is what trips most people up.

A · TEK

The community's own voice

Community-authored or co-produced documents (tribal cultural-resource plans, harvest guidelines, stewardship protocols, tribal health publications), Sámi parliament guidance, commons and customary-use bylaws, official tribal or community videos, oral traditions and myths told in the community's voice, academic TEK papers written in that voice or with named community co-authors, and classical texts of a living tradition when you frame them as that culture's own knowledge.

Counts as a TEK source. Run the validation checklist before you accept the label.

B · Ethnobotany

An observer documenting use

Primary peer-reviewed field studies (Journal of Ethnopharmacology, Economic Botany, Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine), early ethnographic accounts, outside scholarship on a living tradition, and the compilations built from all of those: Moerman's Native American Ethnobotany, USDA NRCS plant guides, Chevallier, Grieve.

Counts as an ethnobotanical source. A compilation counts as a starting point; trace it to the fieldwork underneath for the finished piece. Never TEK, however rich.

C · Peer-reviewed, not ethnobotany

A real paper about something else

Pharmacology or phytochemistry reviews with a "Traditional uses" paragraph, review chapters in clinical handbooks, clinical trials whose introduction mentions traditional use, trade and market studies (even in an ethnobotany journal). Scholarly, citable, and not a study of how a people use the plant.

Counts as neither. Its traditional-uses paragraph is a map: follow the citations in it to the field study, and cite that.

D · Not a source for this section

Synthesis, orientation, or a different story

Herbalist textbooks on their own (they synthesise the primary record rather than generate it), encyclopedias and Wikipedia as citations, hospital health-library pages, 19th-century pharmacy and Eclectic sources, blogs, vendor pages.

Counts as neither. Use these to orient, then follow their citations outward.

Six rules people miss

  • Rich is not the same as right. Moerman's database is enormous and reliable. It is still an outsider's record of what people were observed or reported to do. That makes it ethnobotany.
  • The journal does not decide. A paper in Economic Botany about export volumes and prices is a trade study. A paper in Phytotherapy Research is pharmacology, even when it opens with two paragraphs of history.
  • A compilation is a map, not a destination. Moerman, the USDA guides, and review papers all cite where they got it. Follow that citation to the field study or the ethnography, and cite that.
  • Honesty stands in for a missing TEK source. If a targeted search finds no community-voice source, say so, define TEK, and state which of your sources are TEK and which are ethnobotanical. That statement does the work. A compilation relabelled as TEK does not.
  • Codified traditions count when you name the stance. Chinese medicine, Āyurveda, Unānī: a classical text or practitioner-community source counts as TEK when you say whose knowledge it is, how it is held and passed on, and that you are reading it on its own terms rather than as data about a culture. That is Robin Wall Kimmerer's framing, traditional knowledge and scientific knowledge as complementary ways of knowing. Outside scholarly commentary on those same traditions is ethnobotany.
  • Native range first. The section centres the culture of the plant's native range. Use by a people who adopted the plant later is worth telling, but it is told second.
The TEK validation checklist

Before you label anything TEK, all four should hold: community authorship or named co-authorship is explicit (for Sámi material the word árbediehtu is a strong signal); practices, protocols, or stewardship are described in cultural context rather than as a bare list of uses; the scope is bounded to a named community, landscape, and plant; and any restricted-knowledge or traditional-knowledge notices are respected.

Spot the voice

Read the passage and decide who is speaking. Community voice says "we". Observer voice reports on "they". A secondary restatement repeats an observer's record at one more remove: a database entry, an agency guide, a review's history paragraph.
