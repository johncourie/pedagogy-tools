An exercise in evidence–claim alignment v1.1

Claim Strength Calibrator

Claims in research writing should match the evidence behind them: no stronger, no weaker, and never so vague that nothing could contradict them. This tool gives you practice reading evidence and placing a claim against it. It is built as a floor for a first course in reading study results; later courses raise it.

Seven positions and one escape hatch

Every claim about evidence can be placed on a line that runs from seriously understated to seriously overstated. The line measures certainty, not enthusiasm. A confident “it does not work” written over thin evidence sits just as far from that evidence as a confident “it works.” Version 1 of this tool had four buttons. The colleagues who tested it found the edges too sharp in two places, and both fixes are here: the scale is now symmetric, and there is a band of tolerance around every key.

Seriously understated−3

A reader of the claim would believe weaker evidence exists than actually does. A Cochrane review becomes “preliminary evidence.” A set of controlled trials becomes “anecdotal reports.” The kind of evidence has been misrepresented, downward.

Markers: “preliminary,” “anecdotal,” “unproven” applied to synthesised trial evidence; a formal safety signal reported as rumour

Understated−2

The reader would still know what kind of evidence exists, but hedges have been added or the scope narrowed without cause. A pooled estimate from nine trials becomes “might help some people.” Understating sounds cautious. It is the same error as overstating with the sign flipped.

Markers: stacked hedges (“might possibly,” “some,” “a few”) on strong findings; severity softened in safety writing

Slightly understated−1

The tone is a notch more tentative than the evidence. Kind and scope are intact. This is inside the tolerance band of a calibrated key, and a calibrated placement is inside the band of this one.

Well-calibrated0

The claim reflects what the evidence shows: its type, its scope, its certainty, and its limitations. Nothing is added and nothing important is left out.

Markers: “In one RCT…” “A pilot study found…” “is associated with…” “among postmenopausal women…” “using a standardized extract…” “insufficient to recommend either way…”

Slightly overstated+1

The tone is a notch more confident than the evidence. “May improve” becomes “appears to improve.” Kind and scope are intact.

Overstated+2

The reader would still know what kind of evidence exists, but a hedge is dropped, a population widened, a preparation generalised, an outcome broadened, or a relative risk reported without its absolute. The evidence is real. The claim stretches it.

Markers: definitive verbs where the source hedged; missing qualifiers (“women” for “postmenopausal women”); “the herb” for the preparation studied

Seriously overstated+3

A reader of the claim would believe stronger evidence exists than actually does. In vitro reads as clinical. An association reads as a cause. A tradition reads as a trial. An uncontrolled pilot reads as an established effect. “Insufficient evidence” reads as “shown not to work.”

Markers: causal verbs on observational data; “clinically proven” from preclinical work; “well-established” from tradition; a confident null from an open question

Too vague to placeoff the line

The claim names no preparation, population, outcome, or magnitude that evidence could match or contradict. “Ginkgo supports cognitive function.” “Turmeric promotes joint health.” This is supplement-label language, written to be unfalsifiable on purpose. It cannot be placed on the line, and saying so is the verdict.

Markers: “supports,” “promotes,” “helps maintain,” plus a body system and nothing else

What separates 2 from 3

The hard edge in version 1 was between overstated and seriously overstated, and the reason it was hard is that degree is arguable. The rule now is about kind, which is not. Ask what kind of evidence a reader of the claim would assume exists. If the reader would get the kind right and only the scope or the certainty wrong, the claim is at ±2 or closer. If the reader would get the kind wrong, the claim is at ±3. The rule runs in both directions: a Cochrane review described as a preliminary signal is −3, because a reader would misjudge what exists. Degree still matters inside each kind, and that is what the band is for.

The tolerance band

Each key comes with a band, normally ±1. A placement inside the band is full credit. One step outside is a near miss and half credit. Further is a miss. The band is where honest disagreement lives. It is wide enough to absorb “I can argue the edge case” and narrow enough not to absorb “I did not read the evidence.” If you land outside the band and still think you are right, tick the disagree box. The run record you copy at the end carries your placement and your rationale, and in class the strongest rationale wins, not the key.

Why vague is off the line

If you placed a label-style claim as overstated, you were not wrong. You were supplying the missing claim yourself, then placing the claim you wrote. The sentence on the page does not make it. “Too vague” is the more useful verdict because it names the fix: say what was found, in whom, with what preparation, at what dose. The tool gives half credit for placing a vague-keyed claim on the line and full credit for calling it vague.

The key is over-heavy on purpose

Nine of the eighteen keys sit on the overstated side, three on the understated side, four at zero, and two off the line. That mirrors the literature. In the Saez–Zucman audit, 97% of the higher-severity errors with a clear direction overstated the paper’s claims. It also means a reflex of always answering “overstated” would score respectably here. The lean number in your results catches that reflex: it reports how far above or below the key your placements averaged.

Next: Failure Modes

Eight ways claims get overstated, two ways they get understated

These are the recurring patterns. Most calibration errors in student monograph writing fall into one or more of them. Naming the pattern makes it easier to catch in your own drafts.

1. Hedge dropping

The source uses cautious language, “may,” “appears to,” “is associated with,” and the student’s version removes it.

Source says

“Valerian root extract may improve subjective sleep quality”

Student writes: “Valerian root improves sleep quality”

Fix: Keep the hedge. If the source hedged, it hedged for a reason. Your job is to report the finding, not to strengthen it.

2. Evidence type escalation

The claim is written at the level of a stronger kind of study than the one cited. Cell studies get clinical language. Uncontrolled pilots get the language of confirmed effects. This is the pattern that moves a claim to +3.

Evidence

In vitro: berberine inhibits CYP3A4 at 50–100 µM

Claim: “Berberine causes clinically significant drug interactions”

Fix: Name the study type in your sentence. “In cell culture, berberine inhibited…” forces the claim to stay at the right level.

3. Population generalization

The study examined a specific group and the claim drops the qualifier, widening it to everyone.

Evidence

RCT in postmenopausal women (n=80): black cohosh reduced hot flash frequency by 26%

Claim: “Black cohosh is effective for menopausal symptoms”

Fix: Carry the population through. If the study only enrolled postmenopausal women, your claim is about postmenopausal women.

4. Preparation conflation

The study tested a specific preparation at a specific dose, and the claim refers to “the herb” as though any preparation would behave the same way.

Evidence

Meta-analysis of EGb 761 (standardized ginkgo extract, 240 mg/day) in dementia patients

Claim: “Ginkgo leaf tea supports memory”

Fix: Name the preparation. A study of EGb 761 is a study of EGb 761, not of ginkgo tea, ginkgo tincture, or ginkgo in general.

5. Correlation → causation

An observational study finds an association, and the claim uses causal language: “prevents,” “causes,” “protects against.” The association may be real. The study cannot tell you why it exists.

Evidence

Prospective cohort: higher dietary turmeric intake associated with lower colorectal cancer rates (OR 0.73)

Claim: “Turmeric consumption prevents colorectal cancer”

Fix: Use associational language for associations. “Higher intake was associated with lower rates” is the claim the study supports.

6. Traditional use → efficacy

A traditional use report documents historical practice. The claim treats that history as evidence the plant works for that purpose.

Evidence

Elderberry used in European folk medicine for colds and influenza for centuries

Claim: “Elderberry is a well-established treatment for upper respiratory infections”

Fix: Report the use, not the conclusion. “Elderberry has a long history of use in European folk medicine for cold and flu symptoms” says the same thing without pretending it says more.

7. Confident null

The evidence says “insufficient to recommend.” The claim says “shown not to work.” Absence of evidence has been reported as evidence of absence. The valence is negative but the error is upward: the claim is more certain than the evidence.

Evidence

Most systematic reviews conclude the evidence for milk thistle in liver disease is insufficient to recommend it for any condition

Claim: “Systematic reviews have shown that milk thistle does not work for liver disease”

Fix: Insufficient evidence means the question is open. Write it as open: “insufficient to support a recommendation either way.” A practitioner reads that correctly: no reason to start, no reason to stop.

8. Label-speak

“Supports,” “promotes,” “helps maintain,” plus a body system. The sentence names no preparation, population, outcome, or dose. It is written so that nothing could contradict it, which also means nothing could support it. Sometimes it hides a weak finding. Sometimes it hides a strong one.

Evidence

Meta-analysis of 8 RCTs: about 1,000 mg/day of curcuminoid extract reduced arthritis pain and improved function versus placebo

Claim: “Turmeric promotes joint health”

Fix: Say what was found. “In pooled trials, about 1,000 mg/day of curcuminoid extract reduced arthritis pain over 8 to 12 weeks” is a claim. “Promotes joint health” is a label.

Two ways claims get understated

9. Hedge stacking and type demotion

Hedges are added to a finding that did not need them, or the kind of evidence is demoted: a Cochrane review called “preliminary,” a pooled estimate called “some suggestion.” It reads as caution. It misrepresents the evidence exactly as overstating does.

Evidence

Cochrane review of 29 RCTs (n=5,489): St. John’s wort more effective than placebo for major depression, comparable to standard antidepressants

Claim: “There is some preliminary evidence suggesting St. John’s wort might have antidepressant properties”

Fix: Match the hedge to the evidence. “A Cochrane review of 29 trials found St. John’s wort more effective than placebo for major depression” is the calibrated sentence, and it is shorter.

10. Safety softening

A formal safety signal is reported as rumour: “liver injury with probable causality” becomes “occasionally mentioned in connection with liver complaints.” Understatement in safety writing is not conservative. The practitioner who reads it will not counsel the client who needs it.

Fix: Report severity, frequency, and the causality assessment as the source did. Soften nothing that a client would need to hear.

Compounding

These patterns often appear together. A single sentence can drop a hedge, generalise the population, and conflate the preparation at once. Each step feels small; together they can move a claim two or three positions. That compounding is why careful writers check each dimension separately, and why the key sometimes says “+3 is defensible” on a case keyed at +2.

Next: Where the Eval Happens

Who is doing the judging?

Every placement you make in this tool happens somewhere. It can happen in your head alone. It can happen in your head with a machine’s placement beside it. It can happen in the machine, with you reading its rationale and deciding whether to sign it. These are three different exercises that train three different things, and the tool asks you to declare which one you are doing before you start.

Human

Trains the reflex

You read, you place, you write the rationale. No AI in the loop. This is the thing that has to fire when you are reading a draft late at night with no tool open. It is the slowest mode and the only one that builds the reflex itself.

Centaur

Trains disagreement

You place first, then paste the case into an AI with the scale (the copy button gives you the prompt), compare, and commit. This trains noticing when the machine’s placement is better argued than yours, and when it is not. The rule that makes it work: place before you ask. If you asked first, you are in Machine mode and should say so.

Machine

Trains the audit

You hand the case to the AI, enter its placement as the answer, and your work is the rationale box: would you sign this? This trains what practitioners will actually do with these tools, which is audit a machine’s calibration judgement rather than produce one. It is not cheating when it is declared. It is cheating when it is not.

Declared, not detected

The tool cannot tell which mode you used. It records what you declare and prints it on the run record. That is not a weakness of the tool. The skill being trained here, matching the strength of what you say to the strength of what you have, starts with saying truthfully where a judgement came from. A placement that came from a machine and is reported as yours is itself an overstated claim.

For students

In a first-semester course this tool is a floor: eighteen placements in Human mode, once, before you summarise your first study. Later courses raise the floor: Centaur mode against real papers, and disagreements with the key argued in class until the strongest rationale wins. Pick the mode your course asked for. If it did not ask, pick Human the first time.

For practitioners

Every claim in a monograph, a product page, or a client handout went through one of these three loci, whether or not anyone named it. The audit described under Why It Matters is a machine locus pointed at your published claims after the fact. The only variable you control is which locus checked them before publication, and whether you can say which one it was.

Next: Why It Matters

Why this matters

Every claim you write is a small act of either precision or distortion. You may never intend to mislead anyone. But the cumulative effect of small overstatements across thousands of monographs, blog posts, and product pages is a body of herbal literature that says more than anyone has shown. That literature then gets cited, summarised, and re-summarised, each pass ratcheting the claims a little higher. By the time a practitioner reads the sentence, it bears no resemblance to the evidence it rests on.

This is how an in vitro study becomes “clinically proven.” This is how a pilot study becomes “well-established.” Not through any single dishonest act. Through a thousand small failures of calibration.

The upward ratchet

Writing exerts a natural upward pressure on claim strength. When you paraphrase, you simplify, and simplification drops qualifiers. When you summarise, you generalise, and generalisation widens populations and preparations. When you want your writing to be readable, you reach for clean declarative sentences, and declarative sentences sound more certain than the evidence warrants. None of this is intentional. All of it is predictable.

The skill this tool practises, reading a claim against its evidence and asking whether the strength matches, is the skill every reviewer and editor applies. Practising it before someone else catches your overstatements is how you become the writer whose work can be trusted.

What the audits show

The Saez–Zucman audit pointed systematic AI analysis at 138 economics papers by two prominent researchers. It found 2,035 problems, of which 147 were serious enough to change a headline number. Among the higher-severity errors with a clear direction, 97% overstated the paper’s claims. Not 60%. Not 75%. Ninety-seven percent.

That asymmetry is not special to economics. It reflects a structural truth: every stage of research communication, writing, editing, publicising, summarising, citing, pushes claims upward. Downward errors get caught because they make the work look less interesting. Upward errors survive because they make the work look more interesting. The only defence is a writer who checks the match between claim and evidence as a matter of habit, before anyone else reads it.

The audit is coming to every DOI

The Saez–Zucman audit was two AI systems, run independently, pointed at 138 papers and recomputing their headline numbers from the authors’ own files. Nothing about that is expensive anymore. Pointing the same procedure at every paper that has a DOI is a budget line and a decision, not a capability waiting to be invented. The decision will be made by someone with a reason: a funder checking a portfolio, a journal screening submissions, a litigant looking for a weak link, a competitor, a graduate student with a free weekend. Within a few years, and plausibly sooner, there will be a table somewhere with a row for every claim you have published and a column for its distance from the evidence under it.

Herbal medicine is a soft target for that table. The literature is small, mostly in English, heavy on secondary summaries of the same few trials, and full of sentences written to sound like more than they are. It will audit quickly, and it will audit badly.

Two things follow. First, the asymmetry means the table will be mostly red on the overstated side, and the writers who are not in it will be the ones who ran the same check on themselves first. Second, the audit is a machine locus. Whether it finds anything in your work depends on which locus checked your claims before they went out, which is the question the previous tab asks you to answer on purpose.

What changed in v1.1 (2026-10-30)
  • Four buttons replaced by a seven-point scale with a tolerance band around each key; a near miss earns half credit, and the results report mean miss and lean.
  • “Too vague to place” added as an off-line verdict, with two keyed cases.
  • The understated side given the same depth as the overstated side: type demotion is −3, hedge stacking and safety softening are −2.
  • The boundary between 2 and 3 given a rule: would the reader misjudge the kind of evidence that exists?
  • The milk thistle case split into a calibrated version (“insufficient either way”) and a confident-null version.
  • A rationale box, a disagree-with-the-key box, and a copyable run record for class discussion.
  • A declared evaluation locus per run: Human, Centaur, or Machine, with a copyable AI prompt in the latter two.
  • Four new cases: peppermint oil for IBS, green tea extract hepatotoxicity, the milk thistle pair, and a curcuminoid label claim. Eighteen in total.
