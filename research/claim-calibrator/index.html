An exercise in evidence–claim alignment

Claim Strength Calibrator

Claims in research writing should match the evidence behind them—no stronger, no weaker. This tool gives you practice reading evidence and judging whether a claim is calibrated to it.

The four levels of claim strength

Every claim you write about evidence falls somewhere on this spectrum. The goal is the first level. The others are common in student writing and in published sources.

Well-calibrated

The claim accurately reflects what the evidence shows—its type, its scope, its certainty, and its limitations. Nothing is added, and nothing important is left out.

Language markers: “In one RCT…” “A pilot study found…” “is associated with…” “among postmenopausal women…” “using a standardized extract…”

Understated

The claim is weaker than the evidence supports. A Cochrane review becomes “preliminary evidence,” a well-replicated finding becomes “some studies suggest.” Understating sounds cautious but misrepresents the evidence just as surely as overstating does.

Language markers: “might…” “some preliminary evidence suggests…” “it is possible that…” applied to strong findings

Overstated

The claim goes beyond what the evidence supports. A hedge is dropped, a population is widened, a preparation is generalized, or a finding is presented as more certain than it is. The evidence is real—the claim just stretches it.

Language markers: definitive verbs (“reduces,” “prevents,” “treats”) where the source used tentative ones; missing qualifiers (“women” instead of “postmenopausal women”); “the herb” instead of the specific preparation studied

Seriously overstated

The claim far exceeds the evidence. An in vitro result becomes clinical advice. An association becomes a cause. A traditional use report becomes proof of efficacy. The evidence exists, but it does not come close to supporting what is written.

Language markers: causal language from observational data (“prevents,” “causes”); “clinically proven” from preclinical work; “well-established treatment” from traditional use alone; multiple escalations compounding in one sentence

The asymmetry

In the Saez–Zucman audit, 97% of the major and moderate errors with a clear direction overstated the paper’s claims. Student writing shows the same asymmetry: the overwhelming majority of calibration errors make claims stronger than the evidence, not weaker. This is not because students are dishonest. It is because every step in the writing process—paraphrasing, summarizing, simplifying, wanting the paper to be interesting—exerts a gentle upward pressure on claim strength. The drift is invisible unless you have trained yourself to see it.

Six ways claims get overstated

These are the recurring patterns. Most overstated claims in student monograph writing fall into one or more of these categories. Learning to name the pattern makes it easier to catch in your own drafts.

1. Hedge dropping

The source uses cautious language—“may,” “appears to,” “is associated with”—and the student’s version removes it.

Source says

“Valerian root extract may improve subjective sleep quality”

Student writes: “Valerian root improves sleep quality”

Fix: Keep the hedge. If the source hedged, it hedged for a reason. Your job is to report the finding, not to strengthen it.

2. Evidence type escalation

The student writes a claim appropriate to a stronger kind of study than the one actually cited. Cell studies get described as though they were clinical evidence. Pilot studies get the language of confirmed findings.

Evidence

In vitro: berberine inhibits CYP3A4 at 50–100 µM

Claim: “Berberine causes clinically significant drug interactions”

Fix: Name the study type in your sentence. “In cell culture, berberine inhibited…” forces the claim to stay at the right level.

3. Population generalization

The study examined a specific group—postmenopausal women, adults with mild-to-moderate dementia, competitive athletes—and the claim drops the qualifier, widening it to everyone.

Evidence

RCT in postmenopausal women (n=80): black cohosh reduced hot flash frequency by 26%

Claim: “Black cohosh is effective for menopausal symptoms”

Fix: Carry the population through. If the study only enrolled postmenopausal women, your claim is about postmenopausal women.

4. Preparation conflation

The study tested a specific preparation—a standardized extract at a specific dose—and the claim refers to “the herb” as though any preparation would work the same way.

Evidence

Meta-analysis of EGb 761 (standardized ginkgo extract, 240 mg/day) in dementia patients

Claim: “Ginkgo supports cognitive function”

Fix: Name the preparation. A study of EGb 761 is a study of EGb 761, not a study of ginkgo leaf tea, ginkgo tincture, or ginkgo in general.

5. Correlation → causation

An observational study finds an association, and the claim uses causal language: “prevents,” “causes,” “protects against.” The association may be real, but the study cannot tell you why it exists.

Evidence

Prospective cohort: higher dietary turmeric intake associated with lower colorectal cancer rates (OR 0.73)

Claim: “Turmeric consumption prevents colorectal cancer”

Fix: Use associational language for associations. “Higher intake was associated with lower rates” is the claim the study supports.

6. Traditional use → efficacy

A traditional use report documents historical practice: people used the plant for a purpose. The claim treats that history as evidence the plant works for that purpose.

Evidence

Elderberry used in European folk medicine for colds and influenza for centuries

Claim: “Elderberry is a well-established treatment for upper respiratory infections”

Fix: Report the use, not the conclusion. “Elderberry has a long history of use in European folk medicine for cold and flu symptoms” says the same thing without pretending it says more.

Compounding

These patterns often appear together. A single sentence can drop a hedge, generalize the population, and conflate the preparation simultaneously. Each step feels small; together they can move a claim two or three levels up the spectrum. That compounding is why careful writers check each dimension separately.

Next: Why It Matters

Why this matters

Every claim you write is a small act of either precision or distortion. You may never intend to mislead anyone. But the cumulative effect of small overstatements across thousands of monographs, blog posts, and product pages is a body of herbal literature that says more than anyone has actually shown. That literature then gets cited, summarized, and re-summarized, each pass ratcheting the claims a little higher. By the time a practitioner reads the sentence, it bears no resemblance to the evidence it rests on.

This is how an in vitro study becomes “clinically proven.” This is how a pilot study becomes “well-established.” Not through any single dishonest act, but through a thousand small failures of calibration.

The upward ratchet

Writing exerts a natural upward pressure on claim strength. When you paraphrase, you tend to simplify, and simplification drops qualifiers. When you summarize, you tend to generalize, and generalization widens populations and preparations. When you want your writing to be readable, you reach for clean, declarative sentences, and declarative sentences sound more certain than the evidence warrants. None of this is intentional. All of it is predictable.

The skill this tool practices—reading a claim against its evidence and asking whether the strength matches—is the same skill every reviewer and editor applies. Practicing it before someone else catches your overstatements is how you become the writer whose work can be trusted.

What the audits show

The Saez–Zucman audit pointed systematic AI analysis at 138 economics papers by two prominent researchers. It found 2,035 problems, of which 147 were serious enough to change a headline number. Among the major and moderate errors with a clear direction, 97% overstated the paper’s claims. Not 60%. Not 75%. Ninety-seven percent.

That asymmetry is not special to economics. It reflects a structural truth: every stage of research communication—writing, editing, publicizing, summarizing, citing—pushes claims upward. Downward errors get caught because they make the work look less interesting. Upward errors survive because they make the work look more interesting. The only defense is a writer who checks the match between claim and evidence as a matter of habit, before anyone else reads it.

The ion cannon on the horizon

The tools to run this kind of audit against any corpus—systematically, at negligible cost—are nearly here. The Saez–Zucman audit was a proof of concept. Within a few years, the same analysis will be pointed at entire fields, including herbal medicine. When it arrives, every overstated claim in every published monograph will be findable, traceable, and quotable. The practitioners whose writing survives that audit will be the ones who learned to calibrate their claims now.
